## the premise

AI is going to enable "function collapse" where departments blend together and cross-domain happens by default.

back-of-house specialty will begin to go away as it becomes less and less important to hire someone to "run Salesforce," for example.

which leaves high-touch, human focused roles with more bandwidth to focus on customer experience.

AI and automation will also enable highly cross-functional roles by default, as the cognitive cost of operating domain-specific infrastructure is outsourced.

in theory, every person's role is the steward the business. but in actuality, each domain's role is to steward their domain. e.g. the training department has to justify itself by creating more training, even if it isn't beneficial to the business or the customer. 

enabling cross-functionality by default allows:

- the salesperson to build the project plan.
- the project manager to do the implementation.
- the implementation specialist to interface with the customer about their ongoing business needs.
- the customer success manager to spearhead development and investigate bugs.
- the support and development teams to understand customer needs without intermediaries.
- the developer to ship marketing materials, training, and product documentation.

and any of these roles can be any other role.

previously, domain specialty has been necessary because each role came with a lot of overhead. understanding and navigating the overhead was just as important as executing the role itself. 

if AI is managing the overhead, increased bandwidth allows the emphasis on "department specialization" to become "business domain specialization", and the most valuable employees will be those that understand the business and customers well enough to create the desired outcomes for them.

it will be less important that a project manager is capable of running a PMP, and more important that they can develop a deep understanding of the customer.

it will become less important to design inter-departmental workflows, protocols, and processes, and more important to design a compelling, competitive, and effective *customer experience*.

## internal tooling

the cost of software development is trending to zero.

this means that software that solves internal business problems either by way of connecting disparate systems or by way of a bespoke, tailored solution are about to become plentiful.

consider each of these examples:
- a custom built CRM that costs the same as an annual Salesforce subscription but doesn't come with the bloat and allows the company to evolve it on demand.
- data pipelines that allow every department's information to flow and connect. Sales teams and CSMs may not share software tools, but their data no longer lives in silos. each role can choose (or build!) tools that meet their needs, and custom integrations can be spun up to allow transparency and information sharing across the company.

## the danger to orgs. 

at no other time have small firms been as nimble and capable as they are now.

a 2-5 person team can now compete with Salesforce, because they can offer a stronger customer experience with fewer resources.

more than that, fifty 2-5 person teams can now compete with Salesforce in every niche out there.

AI-native operators are not restricted to a single domain/department, which allows

if a small team can do a needs analysis and ship a product before a large team can finish the sales funnel and project kickoff, then the large team is dead.

and, again, more than that, if every "department" at a small company is aware of the customer's entire business context, sales journey, pain points, onboarding path, and ongoing issues, then customer experience becomes unbeatable.

## adaptive process

if AI and custom software is managing business process overhead and, it matters less that the processes are followed and more that they are functional. if a process introduces friction, workarounds will be developed that circumvent the process in order to solve a problem. under an AI-assisted paradigm, it is suddenly possible to change the processes AS the workaround and have them propagate throughout the company instantly.

continual change is the most important thing. don't create workarounds, implement solutions. put decision rights (and the tools to implement effective change) into the hands of those that the issues affect directly.

one key to this is making all of the processes extremely legible. all policies, SOPs, pipelines, sales funnels, development workflows, etc. should live in plaintext and be accessible to every employee. all AI agents should have access to these documents so that the information is democratized. they should be git versioned, and employees should be scoped to decision right to edit these processes with propagate to the rest of the company in real time and are then followed/enforced/utilized by other agents. we publish engineering blog posts on tuesday instead of wednesday now? great, update the policy repo and the tools adapt automatically. new branding assets? great, update the policy repo and the tools adapt automatically.

## the embedded engineer is the new process consultant.

if adaptability is the most important thing, then the culture must change. consultants don't change the culture. they change the processes, and then they leave. the processes need to be living functions of the business. the business internals need to be undergoing continual evolution.

so an internal initiative is the only viable solution.

but businesses are generally more focused on their business problems than on the state of technology. it takes significant resources and time to do your job AND spearhead an AI-driven transformation. 

we can apply the concept of "forward deployed engineer" here, though. the external perspective is still helpful because it is the knowledge domain of the consultant to understand the problem space as well as the solution potentials. it seems today that AI initiatives so far are focused on AI integration itself, augmenting existing processes. they don't seem to be considering that the organization itself can completely reorganize.

but while consultants have knowledge about the solution potentials, they lack knowledge about the individual business's problems. 

enter: the embedded engineer. an agency (like The Ready) staffs an engineer. the org hires the firm to bring the embedded engineer into the company and work like an employee with an emphasis on org transformation and solution development. the engineer is connected to a network of knowledgeable professionals that are sharing solutions and staying up-to-date on the AI stuff. they build custom solutions for employees and teach the process to others.

## how to implement org change in this context.

i see three directions:

1. existing business processes are iteratively improved by developing custom software solutions at the friction points between departments and within individual processes. the organization remains largely intact, but gets more efficient.
2. a new business unit is spun off to essentially rebuild the business from scratch under the new AI paradigm. an expert from each department that already sees the value and importance of org change is selected for the new team and they take six months to build a new way of working that is inherently cross-functional, and building their own tools and processes, and piloting the solutions. when they achieve it, another team is selected, and the process is repeated, slowly transitioning the entire org into the new shape.
3. an internal change department. a group of junior-ish engineers, or at least technically-savvy employees, that are tasked with roaming each department in search of problems and developing internal tooling for them.

- how this changes org design.

---

i keep saying that AI drives this, but the real trend that underlies this is that the cost of custom software is trending to zero. these solutions do not have to have AI injected into them. but building bespoke solutions to niche business needs is something that was not viable a year ago. you couldn't just spin up a tool in a day for the implementation team to batch upload form data faster. that was a project that required needs analysis and engineering time. but Claude Code could enable a savvy implementation team to build the tool themselves.

## skills i see necessary for making all of this happen successfully

- org design skills. you have to be able to imagine what a company could look like under a variety of contexts in order to shape something that will be productive and effective.
- people and change management skills. orgs are people, they are not org charts. understanding how people will react to change and helping to facilitate it is critical to success.
- tool development and rapid iteration skills. how quickly can you build a prototype? how well can you make it maintainable and iterable? the faster you can solve any specific pain point, the more trust you gain within the org broadly, and the more momentum you build. 


---

- change small things as a mode of building momentum. learning to change small things and getting comfortable with the process becomes a muscle and then that muscle becomes strong enough to tackle larger change. building little tools becomes a confidence builder and then small frictions have been solved and only larger problems become available.
- org design fails because change management has no *durable infrastructure*. it has nowhere to live. 


it comes down to: what becomes the REAL SERVICE.
and can you be successful in supplying it?

keys:
- change mgmt fails because employees don't believe they have any real authority or control or ability to overcome their friction. giving them the authority to solve problems in their ecosystem of 1 and providing them with the tools and skills to do so generates momentum and ENTHUSIASM.
- GROW. CAPABILITY. snowball the capacity for change. as you solve problems in your ecosystem of 1, you naturally begin to extend to an ecosystem of 2, and then your shared role, and then your department, and then interdepartmental. and it extends as individual capacity for change grows. it doesn't percolate into the org from the top, a "culture of improving the way we work" is an expansion of developing the individual skill of improving the way you work.
- it doesn't start from a "change management initiative" or "org design department", it starts from developing one single new skill, building confidence in your ability to influence the world around you, and TRUSTing that the organization itself has your back in this process.
